Release 4.2: From Product Failure to Product Improvement

A 30-second cut to the ping wait quietly pushed four responders out of work, and nobody (not the system, not their handlers, not us) noticed for weeks.

→My recommendation: don't just turn the dial back to 90 seconds. Fix the rules underneath, and make sure no responder can go quiet again without their handler knowing.

Executive recommendation

No one goes quiet without anyone knowing

Engineering says the code fix is small. The Director of Product asked for something a handler would notice and a responder would feel, "not a setting". So the recommendation is three changes, two of which people can see.

2 · A straight answer on the phone

What went wrong with Release 4.2

The release did what it said, and that's the problem

What 4.2 set out to do (12 Aug)

  • Proximity weighted up; recent acceptance weighted down.
  • Ping wait cut from 90 to 60 seconds.
  • Saved filters persist.
  • The notes for handlers left out "recent acceptance down".

What actually happened

  • Answers that used to take 60–90 s became misses, and a miss is scored like a turn-down.
  • Four responders sank to the bottom and stopped being asked. They can't earn their way back.
  • The headline acceptance rate fell from 77% to 54%, then "recovered" to 73% only because the four were no longer pinged.
  • Support tickets went from 6–8 a week to 20–32. All 45 routing tickets are still open.

The product solution

Every change traces back to a finding

Change 1 · visible

Handler heads-up

  • "Vesper hasn't been asked in 5 days (usually about 12 pings a week)."
  • A warning after 2 missed pings in a row.
  • Busy cards flagged; an area split for multi-responder handlers.

Because: Dot never knew; tickets cover 1 in 7 misses; Kip's two cards "might as well be two different products".

Change 2 · visible

A straight answer on the phone

  • "This one moved on after 60 seconds. A timeout doesn't count against you."
  • "You're first in line for the next callout that suits you."

Because: 13 "gone before I could answer" tickets; responders left guessing why the phone went silent.

Change 3 · underneath

Fairer rules

  • A timeout is not a "no": only a real no lowers the score.
  • Scores drift back toward 0.5 over a few weeks.
  • 5 days without a ping puts someone first in line for the next suitable callout.

Because: offer.py:30, no recovery, routing stops at the first yes.

Why not just set the wait back to 90 s?

It would repeat the mistake: quietly changing one number. The rules underneath would still push the next slow week's responders out, silently. And with no answer times, nobody knows whether 90 s is right either.

Trade-offs we accept

  • The busiest responders lose some work; their handlers need warning.
  • No raw scores shown to handlers: a warning they can act on beats a number they'd have to interpret.
  • Warnings name responders by responder name only. Nothing stores or hints at who they are.

Prototype deep dive

Seven steps, two people, one screen each

Aunt Dot's console on the left, Vesper's phone on the right. Here's what each step shows, and what's real.

  1. Today: nothing looks wrong. Card says "Available". Last ping 1 Sep, last take 17 Aug. Phone silent.
  2. Dot gets a heads-up. "Vesper has gone quiet… now first in line." One button: Let Vesper know.
  3. Dot lets Vesper know. Phone: "You're first in line for the next callout that suits you."
  4. A ping arrives. 60-second countdown (3× in the demo). Let it run out, or take it.
  5. A timeout is not a "no". Both screens explain it. Today this would have cost 0.12.
  6. The next suitable callout. Real-speed clock. Tap Take it.
  7. Vesper is back. Warning clears on its own.

What's functional, simulated or proposed

  • functional Step navigation, the alerts, the live countdown, timeout and take paths, the activity log.
  • simulated The callout, the clock and the console are scripted. No routing or scoring runs.
  • proposed Wording, the 5-day and 2-miss thresholds and placement, for the product designer to test with handlers.

Lessons learned

What this taught me about doing the job

Distrust the average

Acceptance "recovered" to 73% while four people were locked out. Asking for the rows, not just the number, was what found them.

Ask what's missing

The biggest findings were absences: no recovery, no alert, no owner, no answer times, an unanswered question from 14 Aug.

Two sources that disagree are a clue

Tickets said "quiet"; interviews said "too fast". Both were true, and the disagreement pointed straight at the cause.

Label what you know

Verified, inferred, open. The root cause is strongly supported, and I still want Wen to confirm it before anyone ships.

Design for the person, not the setting

The fix that matters is the one Dot and Vesper would notice. A config change alone would have left them in the dark again.

Make the lesson repeatable

Claude Code sped up reading, querying and building. Turning the lesson into a skill is what makes it last beyond me.

Final executive takeaway

Fix the rules, and make silence visible.

  1. 4.2 revealed that a sensible-looking change can silently shut people out when the rules underneath punish slowness forever.
  2. The investigation established that misses, not refusals, sank 4 of 16 responders, and it wasn't seasonal (Wen to confirm the code).
  3. The solution: a handler heads-up, a straight answer on the phone, and fairer rules: timeouts aren't a no, scores recover, five quiet days means first in line.
  4. The prototypes let you feel it as Aunt Dot and as Kip, in seconds.
  5. review-checklist catches missing owners, missing measures and drifting scope before a brief is built.
  6. The decision I need: name an owner, and have Wen confirm the mechanism before anything ships.
